MATH 131A · Real Analysis I

Uniform continuity

defanalysis/continuity

Let f:(X,dX)→(Y,dY) be a map between metric spaces.

Definition

∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 ∀x,y∈X:dX(x,y)<δ⟹dY(f(x),f(y))<ε.

One δ=δ(ε) works everywhere. For ordinary continuity, δ may also depend on the base point.

Lipschitz⟹uniformly continuous⟹continuous.

Heine–Cantor

A continuous map from a compact metric space to a metric space is uniformly continuous.

Proof by contradiction: if not, choose xn,yn with dX(xn,yn)<1/n,dY(f(xn),f(yn))≥ε0. Compactness gives xnk→x∈X, hence ynk→x. Continuity and the triangle inequality contradict the lower bound.

Consequences and their hypotheses

  • Uniformly continuous maps send Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences, whether or not a limit lies in the domain.
  • If E⊆Rd is bounded and f:E→R uniformly continuous, then f is bounded. Reason: cover E by finitely many balls at the δ-scale for ε=1.
  • For finite a<b, f:(a,b)→R has a continuous extension to [a,b] iff f is uniformly continuous. Define endpoint values by limits of Cauchy images; completeness of R gives existence, uniform continuity gives independence of the approaching sequence. The extension is unique and uniformly continuous.

General metric caution: bounded domain alone is insufficient for bounded image. On N with discrete metric d(m,n)=1 for m≠n, f(n)=n is uniformly continuous but unbounded. The finite-cover argument needs total boundedness (a finite δ-net for every δ>0).

Strict implications: x on [0,1] is uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz; 1/x on (0,1) is continuous but not uniformly continuous.
